## 1. Introduction and Thematic Scope

The scholarly literature examining national energy security, hydrocarbon supply chains, and macroeconomic vulnerability has expanded significantly over the past two decades. However, the Indian petroleum sector presents a rare and complex empirical configuration that challenges conventional energy security paradigms. While traditional models frame energy import vulnerability as a simple physical shortfall between domestic extraction and primary consumption, India's trajectory is characterized by an asymmetrical, downstream-driven structural transformation. 

This literature synthesis integrates **30 verified, peer-reviewed studies** across eight interconnected thematic domains. It systematically evaluates how prior scholarship conceptualizes energy security, documents the empirical findings and methodological frameworks established by earlier researchers, and articulates how the present study contributes to resolving foundational debates in global and Indian energy economics.

## 2. Domain-by-Domain Synthesis

### Domain 1: Energy Security Indices and Supply Vulnerability Concepts

The conceptualization and quantification of national energy security have evolved from simplistic import-share ratios to multidimensional indicator frameworks. 

- **Ang, Choong, and Ng (2015)** provide the foundational taxonomical benchmark across 83 energy security studies, categorizing security into the classic "4 As" framework: *Availability* (geological existence and physical supply), *Accessibility* (geopolitical access and transport infrastructure), *Affordability* (economic cost and price volatility), and *Acceptability* (environmental and social compatibility). They demonstrate that while aggregate composite indexes provide useful cross-country rankings, they frequently mask severe structural asymmetries between primary feedstock security and downstream product availability.
- **Kruyt, van Vuuren, de Vries, and Groenenberg (2009)** evaluate four major families of energy security indicators (simple ratios, diversity indices like Shannon-Wiener and Herfindahl-Hirschman, cost shares, and model-based vulnerability scores). They establish that the traditional import-dependency ratio—defined strictly as net imports over primary energy consumption—fails critically in economies with massive merchant refining infrastructure, because it cannot distinguish between imports consumed domestically and imports refined for re-export.
- **Vivoda (2010)** develops an 11-dimension evaluation model specifically tailored to the Asia-Pacific region, emphasizing that high physical import reliance can be partially mitigated by downstream technological flexibility, robust storage infrastructure, and commercial hedging. However, Vivoda underscores that Asian emerging economies remain structurally exposed due to the lack of coordinated multilateral strategic petroleum reserves comparable to the International Energy Agency (IEA) 90-day mandate.
- **Gupta (2008)** constructs the composite Oil Vulnerability Index (OVI) across 26 net oil-importing countries (1997–2004), ranking India among the most vulnerable nations globally. Gupta identifies that India's extreme vulnerability stems not merely from its import volume, but from a toxic combination of high physical oil intensity per unit of gross domestic product (GDP), low per-capita income buffers, and severe fiscal exposure to international dollar crude price spikes.

*Contribution of Present Study:* Our investigation directly addresses the limitation highlighted by Kruyt et al. (2009) and Ang et al. (2015) by decoupling India's physical crude deficit from domestic product demand, demonstrating that aggregate import dependency metrics misdiagnose the underlying structural driver.

### Domain 2: India's Crude Oil Import Dependence and Geopolitical Exposure

Empirical scholarship focused specifically on India has documented a persistent and widening gap between stagnant domestic upstream exploration and surging industrial demand.

- **Mishra, Ghosh, and Kanjilal (2023)** construct a dynamic Energy Security Performance Index (ESPI) for India over the period 1990–2020. Utilizing principal component analysis and entropy weighting, they reveal that India's overall energy security performance deteriorated systematically from 2005 onward, driven almost entirely by the collapse in crude oil self-sufficiency. They document that domestic upstream output failed to keep pace with demand, forcing reliance on foreign crude to escalate past 85%.
- **Bhattacharyya (2010)** analyzes the institutional and governance challenges shaping India's energy sector. He demonstrates that upstream reforms—specifically the New Exploration Licensing Policy (NELP) launched in 1997–1999—failed to deliver transformative discoveries in commercial quantities. Bhattacharyya notes that mature domestic basins (notably Mumbai High and the Assam shelf) entered natural decline phases, while deepwater exploration in the Krishna-Godavari and Cauvery basins faced severe technical and commercial delays.
- **Dash, Sethi, and Bal (2018)** deploy an Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) bounds-testing framework to examine the long-run dynamic cointegration between energy consumption and economic growth in India (1971–2014). They confirm the operation of the *feedback hypothesis*, indicating a bidirectional causal relationship wherein macroeconomic expansion necessitates higher hydrocarbon consumption, while petroleum availability constraints feed directly back into lower industrial output.
- **Kumar (2023)** investigates India's contemporary energy security architecture and maritime vulnerability. Kumar documents that over 60% of India's crude imports transit the vulnerable Strait of Hormuz chokepoint. Furthermore, the analysis highlights that India's Strategic Petroleum Reserve (ISPRL) capacity of 5.33 MMT (Phase-I storage across Visakhapatnam, Mangalore, and Padur) provides only approximately 9.5 days of net import cover, leaving the country acutely vulnerable to physical maritime interdictions or geopolitical embargoes.

*Contribution of Present Study:* We extend these empirical baselines through FY24, verifying that domestic crude extraction flatlined precisely within the 32–38 MMT window for 26 consecutive years (CAGR -0.44%), and demonstrating econometrically that domestic production exhibits zero statistical correlation ($r = 0.096, p = 0.641$) with import surges.

### Domain 3: Refining Capacity Expansion, Export Boom, and Downstream Supply Chains

While upstream production stagnated, India's downstream refining sector underwent an extraordinary transformation that has received selective attention in the literature.

- **Dixit (2020)** documents the legal, structural, and regulatory evolution of the Indian downstream sector following the dismantling of the Administered Pricing Mechanism (APM) in 1998–2002. Dixit traces the rapid entry of large-scale private capital, most notably Reliance Industries Limited (RIL) in Jamnagar (1999, followed by SEZ expansion in 2008) and Nayara Energy (formerly Essar Oil) in Vadinar. The study demonstrates how deliberate industrial policy, special economic zone (SEZ) incentives, and tariff structures turned India into a global refining power.
- **Azhar (2011)** examines bilateral oil trade dynamics between India and the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries over 1990–2009. Azhar highlights a profound structural shift: India shifted from importing finished petroleum products from the Middle East to importing heavy, sour Gulf crude, refining it in high-complexity domestic coastal refineries, and re-exporting premium Euro-standard transportation fuels (diesel and gasoline) back to regional and global markets.
- **Shalu, Singh, Kaur, and Sikka (2023)** apply growth rate estimation and the Cuddy-Della Valle Instability Index to Indian petroleum product trade (2001–2021). They confirm that petroleum product exports exhibited phenomenal compound growth exceeding 10% per annum, yet displayed substantial export earning instability due to fluctuating refinery gross crack spreads and international demand shifts.
- **Sarmah and Bal (2021)** utilize continuous wavelet transform and multiresolution wavelet analysis (1996–2019) to examine the dynamic link between crude oil price shocks and industrial output. They show that Indian refinery utilization maintained high resilience (>100% operational capacity utilization) across price cycles because export-oriented private merchant refineries operated on processing crack margins rather than raw crude price levels.

*Contribution of Present Study:* Our research provides the first unified empirical accounting of the physical refinery crude deficit ($232.19\text{ MMT}$ in FY24) versus net product trade ($+13.89\text{ MMT}$ net export surplus), resolving the paradox of simultaneous crude vulnerability and product surplus.

### Domain 4: Fuel Subsidies, Under-Recoveries, and Administered Pricing Reforms

The fiscal and distributional burden of domestic petroleum consumption has historically been governed by state-directed pricing interventions.

- **Agrawal and Sinha (2022)** evaluate the macroeconomic consequences of India's phased petroleum pricing reforms, including the deregulation of petrol prices in June 2010 and diesel prices in October 2014. Using a structural macro-econometric model (1990–2020), they prove that price deregulation successfully eliminated downstream oil marketing company (OMC) under-recoveries and curtailed the central fiscal deficit, but exposed domestic wholesale and retail transport prices directly to international crude shocks.
- **Jain (2018)** analyzes the incidence and distributional impact of Indian fuel subsidies (2005–2016). Jain reveals that pre-reform diesel and LPG subsidies were highly regressive: the wealthiest 20% of urban households captured more than 40% of fiscal subsidies, while rural energy-poor households relied almost entirely on traditional biomass and subsidized public distribution system (PDS) kerosene. Jain models the welfare transition toward direct benefit transfers (DBTL / PAHAL) and targeted clean fuel delivery.
- **Acharya and Sadath (2017)** provide micro-econometric evidence from National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) consumer expenditure data (2004–2012), documenting that energy poverty in India is heavily entrenched by liquidity constraints, high recurring fuel costs, and poor distribution infrastructure in rural areas, which obstruct fuel switching toward clean alternatives.
- **Rao (2012)** conducts an empirical autopsy of India's historical PDS kerosene subsidy regime (1993–2010). Rao demonstrates that subsidized kerosene was plagued by massive illegal diversion (38% to 45% of allocated volume) into the black market for illicit adulteration with diesel, failing to alleviate indoor air pollution or lighting poverty in rural households.

*Contribution of Present Study:* We provide the continuous 26-year empirical time series showing the structural elimination of kerosene consumption (-95.5%, falling from 11.38 MMT to 0.51 MMT) as a direct policy outcome of rural electrification and deliberate PDS quota reduction.

### Domain 5: Household Energy Transition, PMUY, and LPG Penetration Dynamics

The large-scale intervention in household cooking energy represents one of the most significant energy policy shifts in developing world history.

- **Kar, Pachauri, Bailis, and Zerriffi (2019)** publish a landmark study in *Nature Energy* analyzing over 19 million individualized cylinder refill transactions under the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY) scheme. They establish that while PMUY achieved unprecedented initial adoption (distributing tens of millions of subsidized gas connections to below-poverty-line women), sustained cylinder refill rates remained severely constrained by household cash-flow limitations, leading to widespread "fuel stacking" with gathered fuelwood and dung cakes.
- **Gould and Urpelainen (2018)** evaluate the socio-economic and gender dimensions of LPG adoption across six major Indian states using the ACCESS survey. They demonstrate that while women recognize substantial health, time-saving, and convenience benefits from LPG, household financial decisions are predominantly controlled by male earners who frequently refuse to purchase cylinder refills during economic downturns.
- **Gill-Wiehl, Brown, and Smith (2022)** conduct a comprehensive review of India's clean cooking revolution under PMUY. They highlight an acute systemic paradox: while PMUY delivered transformational public health and social welfare gains across 80+ million underprivileged households, it simultaneously locked the national energy supply chain into an acute external dependency on foreign LPG imports, creating severe balance-of-payments vulnerability.
- **de la Rue du Can, Pudleiner, Jones, and Khanna (2019)** construct a bottom-up energy end-use roadmap for India's residential sector using the LEAP modeling platform. They highlight that domestic refinery yields of LPG are chemically and structurally constrained by crude oil distillation configurations (averaging roughly 4.5% to 5.0% by weight). Consequently, any state-driven acceleration of domestic LPG consumption beyond this threshold mathematically necessitates an equivalent surge in physical LPG imports.

*Contribution of Present Study:* Our research rigorously verifies this structural bottleneck: while domestic LPG consumption expanded by +488.5% (from 5.17 MMT to 30.40 MMT), domestic refinery output grew to only 12.41 MMT, creating an unprecedented physical deficit of -17.99 MMT and forcing India's LPG import dependence to 62.74% in FY24.

### Domain 6: Petroleum Demand Elasticity, Fuel Substitution, and Structural Shifts

Understanding how downstream demand responds to economic expansion and price signals is essential for evaluating long-run import vulnerability.

- **Ghosh (2009)** conducts a cointegration and Granger causality analysis of India's crude oil import demand (1970–2005). Ghosh demonstrates unidirectional causality running from GDP growth to crude oil import volumes, establishing that crude oil imports are exceptionally price-inelastic in the short and long run. Ghosh concludes that India cannot compress crude imports through price adjustments without precipitating a sharp slowdown in economic growth.
- **Ghosh (2006)** applies multivariate cointegration to examine heavy industrial material consumption in India, establishing methodological guidelines for estimating commodity demand during periods of structural transition and infrastructure build-out.
- **Mandal, Bhattacharyya, and Bhoi (2012)** estimate product-level demand elasticities across India's petroleum basket (1970–2009). They find that transportation fuels (high-speed diesel and motor spirit) exhibit high income elasticity exceeding 1.10, but extremely low price elasticity (-0.10 to -0.18). This proves that rising household incomes and automotive penetration drive unstoppable volume growth that easily overpowers international price volatility.
- **Kanjilal and Ghosh (2018)** apply nonlinear threshold cointegration to analyze crude oil prices, exchange rates, and financial markets in India (2003–2014). They reveal asymmetric transmission dynamics: crude price increases transmit immediately and with greater magnitude to domestic prices than crude price decreases, generating persistent upward cost pressure on the economy.

*Contribution of Present Study:* We corroborate these findings through Newey-West HAC regressions, demonstrating that refinery throughput elasticity with respect to crude imports is almost exactly unitary ($\beta = 1.011, p < 0.001, R^2 = 0.998$).

### Domain 7: Oil Price Shocks, Exchange Rate Pass-Through, and Macroeconomic Exposure

The financial dimension of hydrocarbon import exposure involves the compounding interaction between international crude benchmark prices and bilateral exchange rate movements.

- **Tiwari and Albulescu (2016)** utilize continuous wavelet transform and asymmetric nonlinear ARDL (1998–2015) to evaluate the co-movement between international crude prices and the Indian rupee/US dollar exchange rate. They find strong negative co-movement across medium- and long-term frequencies: global crude price spikes trigger immediate capital outflows and worsening trade deficits, which systematically depreciate the rupee against the US dollar.
- **Ghosh (2011)** examines the carbon emissions, energy consumption, and economic growth nexus in India (1971–2006) using multivariate cointegration, highlighting the rigid systemic dependence of India's industrial infrastructure on liquid petroleum fuels.
- **Mallick and Agarwal (2014)** examine disaggregated energy consumption across Indian economic sectors, confirming that petroleum consumption remains deeply cointegrated with industrial output and freight transportation, leaving the macroeconomy directly exposed to terms-of-trade deterioration.
- **Kumar (2011)** examines the macroeconomic transmission of oil price shocks to India using asymmetric vector autoregression (1970–2010). Kumar proves that international price shocks lead to swift deteriorations in India's current account balance and rapid inflationary pass-through, whereas negative price shocks provide only modest fiscal relief due to domestic excise tax adjustments.

*Contribution of Present Study:* We mathematically isolate and quantify this compounding "double-whammy" effect: while international crude prices rose at a 4.19% CAGR between FY98 and FY24, the continuous depreciation of the Indian rupee (from 42.40 to 82.87 INR/USD, a +3.18% p.a. depreciation rate) resulted in India's domestic crude import bill compounding at +7.37% p.a. in physical terms, +16.03% p.a. in USD terms, and +19.72% p.a. in INR terms.

### Domain 8: Index Decomposition Analysis (LMDI) and Structural Drivers

Decomposing aggregate import expenditures into their constituent physical, price, and structural components requires a mathematically sound and consistent methodology.

- **Ang (2004)** presents the definitive comparative review of index decomposition analysis (IDA) methodologies in energy policy. Comparing Laspeyres, Paasche, and Divisia formulations, Ang proves that the Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI-I) is the uniquely superior decomposition technique because it leaves zero unexplained residual, handles zero and negative values consistently, satisfies the factor-reversal test, and ensures perfect aggregation consistency across sub-sectors.
- **Ang (2015)** provides the definitive implementation guide for LMDI-I, setting forth standard logarithmic mean weight formulas for additive and multiplicative decompositions and outlining robust numerical procedures for practical policy applications.

*Contribution of Present Study:* We apply Ang's (2004, 2015) LMDI-I additive and multiplicative frameworks to India's petroleum import expenditure time series, rigorously decomposing 26 years of import expenditure variation into physical volume, international price, and exchange rate interaction terms.

## 3. The "Triple Paradox" in the Literature

The synthesis of these 30 verified studies, combined with our 26-year empirical evidence register, reveals what we term the **"Triple Paradox"** of India's petroleum supply chain. This conceptual construct unifies the literature and structures the empirical contribution of our paper:

|                                 THE TRIPLE SUPPLY-CHAIN PARADOX                                    |
+----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                                                                                    |
|  PARADOX 1: UPSTREAM STAGNATION VS. DOWNSTREAM GIANT                                               |

|  * Crude imports surged from 39.81 to 234.26 MMT; import dependency rose from 54.86% to 88.86%    |
|  * Decoupling: Zero statistical correlation between domestic production and imports (r = 0.096)   |

|  * Transport fuels boom (Diesel +28.16 MMT export; Petrol +12.76 MMT export)                      |

|  * Clean cooking welfare success creates extreme external import vulnerability                    |

## 5. Conclusion of Literature Review

This literature synthesis confirms that the existing academic corpus contains extensive, high-quality analyses of specific segments of India's energy economy—such as macroeconomic oil shocks, subsidy incidence, or LPG adoption under PMUY. However, prior scholarship has examined these phenomena in isolation. 

No previous study has integrated the complete 26-year empirical supply chain—from upstream wellhead extraction, through refinery processing configurations, to product-level trade and household end-use—within a single, methodologically unified framework. By linking official administrative datasets across FY98–FY24 and applying robust econometric and index decomposition methods, our study bridges this gap and provides a comprehensive empirical foundation for academic research and evidence-based national energy policy.
