## 1. Executive Summary

Despite a voluminous literature on India's energy sector, empirical scholarship exhibits critical blind spots, methodological fragmentation, and outdated assumptions regarding the structural mechanics of the country's hydrocarbon supply chain. Most existing studies either examine macroeconomic oil price shocks in isolation (e.g., Tiwari & Albulescu, 2016; Kumar, 2011), focus narrowly on upstream reserve depletion (e.g., Bhattacharyya, 2010), or evaluate individual social programs such as PMUY at a microeconomic household level (e.g., Kar et al., 2019). 

Consequently, conventional academic discourse frequently relies on aggregated, high-level narratives that conflate crude oil deficits with finished petroleum product dependency. This paper resolves five fundamental literature gaps by providing the first unified, end-to-end empirical analysis of India's petroleum supply chain across 26 continuous financial years (FY98–FY24).

### Gap 1: Supply Chain Fragmentation and Lack of End-to-End Accounting

*The Literature Deficit:*  
Existing studies routinely examine individual nodes of the petroleum supply chain in silos. Upstream analysts study exploration acreage and geological depletion (Bhattacharyya, 2010); macroeconomists study oil price shocks, exchange rate volatility, and GDP growth (Ghosh, 2009; Sarmah & Bal, 2021); and development economists study household fuel switching and rural subsidies (Jain, 2018; Kar et al., 2019). No published study integrates upstream extraction, cross-border crude logistics, domestic refining throughput, intra-product transformation, product-level export/import flows, and domestic sectoral consumption into a single, mass-balanced empirical framework.

*Our Paper's Resolution:*  
We establish a continuous, verified 26-year dataset (FY98–FY24) that tracks hydrocarbon flows across every stage of the supply chain. By harmonizing MoPNG, PPAC, and DGCI&S administrative data, we construct an end-to-end accounting model that links upstream field production directly to final retail consumption and export destinations.

---

### Gap 2: Conflation of Primary Feedstock Deficit with Refined Product Dependency

*The Literature Deficit:*  
The prevailing narrative in international energy policy and media characterizes India simply as an "energy-dependent nation importing nearly 90% of its petroleum." Authors such as Mishra et al. (2023) and Gupta (2008) utilize high-level import ratios that fail to differentiate between raw crude oil and finished petroleum products. This conflation obscures the fact that India is simultaneously one of the world's most vulnerable crude oil buyers and one of the world's largest exporters of refined transport fuels.

*Our Paper's Resolution:*  
We explicitly decouple the upstream feedstock balance from downstream product trade. We show that while India's crude import dependency escalated from 54.86% in FY98 to 88.86% in FY24 (creating a physical crude deficit of 232.19 MMT), India has maintained an uninterrupted net *export surplus* in refined petroleum products every single year since FY02 (+13.89 MMT in FY24, peaking at +43.08 MMT in FY14). We establish that India operates as an offshore refining clearinghouse, importing foreign crude to export high-value fuels.

### Gap 3: Neglect of Intra-Product Structural Asymmetries (Transport Fuel Surplus vs. Clean Cooking Deficit)

*The Literature Deficit:*  
Studies that do disaggregate product trade (e.g., Shalu et al., 2023; Azhar, 2011) typically analyze aggregate product totals or focus exclusively on diesel and gasoline. Conversely, public health and energy access literature (e.g., Kar et al., 2019; Gould & Urpelainen, 2018; Gill-Wiehl et al., 2022) focuses on PMUY adoption rates without connecting cooking gas to national refinery engineering limits and external import exposure.

*Our Paper's Resolution:*  
We demonstrate that India's product trade is internally split into two opposite regimes:
1. *A massive transport fuel structural surplus:* Diesel net exports reached +28.16 MMT, petrol reached +12.76 MMT, and aviation turbine fuel reached +8.58 MMT in FY24.
2. *An acute, structural domestic clean cooking deficit:* Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG) consumption surged by +488.5% (from 5.17 MMT to 30.40 MMT) under PMUY and urban expansion. Because domestic refinery LPG yield is chemically bounded at ~4.8% of crude throughput, domestic LPG production reached only 12.41 MMT. This created an unavoidable physical deficit of -17.99 MMT, driving LPG import dependency to 62.74%. 

Our study provides the first econometric and technical integration showing how social policy successes in clean cooking directly exacerbate external balance-of-payments vulnerability.

### Gap 4: Misattribution of Import Drivers (The Crowding-Out Fallacy)

*The Literature Deficit:*  
A widespread assumption in policy discussions and academic introductions is that India's surging crude oil imports are the direct consequence of "failing domestic production." This implies an economic crowding-out hypothesis: if domestic exploration had succeeded, crude imports would have been curtailed proportionally.

*Our Paper's Resolution:*  
We conduct rigorous statistical and econometric testing (Pearson correlation, first-difference correlation, and Newey-West HAC time-series regressions) to refute this hypothesis. We prove:
- Domestic crude production did not collapse; it remained flat within an extraordinarily narrow band of 32.03 to 37.81 MMT across 26 years (CAGR -0.44%).
- Crude imports expanded nearly sixfold from 39.81 to 234.26 MMT (CAGR +7.37%).
- The correlation between domestic production and crude imports is statistically indistinguishable from zero ($r = 0.096, p = 0.641$; first differences $r = 0.173, p = 0.408$).
- Regressing crude imports on domestic production yields an insignificant coefficient ($\beta = -0.320, p = 0.271$).
- Conversely, refinery throughput grew by +281.6% (from 68.54 to 261.55 MMT), and throughput regressed on crude imports exhibits a near-perfect unitary elasticity ($\beta = 1.011, p < 0.001, R^2 = 0.998$).

This definitively establishes that crude imports were pulled into India by downstream refining capacity additions, not pushed by domestic upstream depletion.

### Gap 5: Absence of Multi-Decadal Physical-Price-Currency LMDI Decomposition

*The Literature Deficit:*  
While Ang (2004, 2015) established the mathematical superiority of the Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) for energy decomposition, existing applications to India's petroleum imports are either cross-sectional, cover short time horizons (e.g., 3–5 years), or decompose energy intensity rather than import expenditure. Studies examining import bills (e.g., Agrawal & Sinha, 2022; Kumar, 2011) fail to formally decompose expenditure into physical volume, international dollar price shocks, and domestic currency depreciation effects simultaneously.

*Our Paper's Resolution:*  
We execute both additive and multiplicative LMDI-I index decompositions over the complete 26-year panel (FY98–FY24). We prove:
- Price shocks represent the dominant source of short-term volatility, explaining 72% of inter-annual expenditure variance ($r = 0.917$ with crude prices).
- Long-run secular growth in import expenditures is driven by volume growth (+7.37% CAGR) compounded by steady currency depreciation (+3.18% p.a., as the rupee moved from 42.40 to 82.87 INR/USD).
- This compound effect explains why India's physical crude imports grew 5.9-fold, while the USD import bill grew 37.9-fold ($3.52B to $133.37B), and the domestic INR burden exploded 74.1-fold (Rs. 14,917 Cr to Rs. 1,105,176 Cr).

## 4. Theoretical Framework and Conceptual Positioning

The paper positions its empirical findings within three overarching theoretical paradigms in energy economics:

```
                                  +-----------------------------------------------+
                                  |     PARADIGM 1: EXTENDED 4-As SECURITY       |
                                  |  Availability (Crude) vs Accessibility (Prod) |
                                  +-----------------------+-----------------------+
                                                          |
                                                          v
+-----------------------------------------------+         |         +-----------------------------------------------+
|     PARADIGM 2: STRUCTURAL INDUSTRIAL POLICY   | <-------+-------> |       PARADIGM 3: TERMS-OF-TRADE SHOCKS       |
| Coastal Export Refining as Global Merchant Hub|                   | Currency Pass-Through & Macro Vulnerability   |
+-----------------------------------------------+                   +-----------------------------------------------+
```

1. **Extended "4 As" Energy Security Framework (Ang et al., 2015):**  
   We demonstrate that energy security cannot be treated as a scalar metric. India possesses high *Accessibility* and downstream *Availability* for refined liquid fuels (refining capacity is 112% of domestic consumption), but suffers from critical *Availability* failure in upstream crude feedstock (88.9% import dependence) and acute *Affordability* risks driven by foreign exchange volatility.

2. **Downstream Industrial Policy and Merchant Refining Hub Dynamics:**  
   Drawing on Dixit (2020) and Azhar (2011), we frame India's refining sector not merely as a domestic utility, but as a deliberate state-supported export industry. Private capital investments (RIL, Nayara) created coastal mega-refineries designed to capture international crack spreads, fundamentally decoupling refinery throughput from domestic fuel absorption.

3. **Macroeconomic Terms-of-Trade and Asymmetric Exchange Rate Pass-Through:**  
   Building on Tiwari & Albulescu (2016) and Kanjilal & Ghosh (2018), we formalize the financial vulnerability mechanism. In an economy with inelastic physical demand for transport and cooking energy, international oil price spikes deteriorate the trade balance, induce domestic currency depreciation, and amplify the domestic cost of energy in a self-reinforcing inflationary loop.

## 5. Strategic Significance for Policy and Scholarly Literature

By addressing these five fundamental literature gaps, the present study redefines the empirical baseline for Indian energy research. It demonstrates that policy initiatives aimed at reducing import dependence cannot succeed through upstream marginal exploration incentives alone. Because crude imports are mathematically bound to refinery throughput, and refinery throughput is driven by export markets and inelastic transport demand, true energy security requires structural interventions: electrifying light mobility, expanding biofuels and green hydrogen, diversifying cooking energy toward piped gas and clean induction, and establishing counter-cyclical financial hedging against foreign exchange depreciation.
